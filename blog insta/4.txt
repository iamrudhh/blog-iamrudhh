They Never Asked For His Password
One cracked download, one café hotspot, one extension — and an account gone in silence
Rahul is a design student and his software budget is zero.

So when a "free full version" of the editing suite he needed appeared in a tutorial video description, he downloaded it. The installer had a banner ad, a couple of extra checkboxes he clicked past, and then the software opened and worked. Job done.

Three weeks later he was in a café, on their free Wi-Fi, installing a Chrome extension that promised to show him who unfollowed him.

A month after that, someone logged into his Instagram from a city he's never been to. No password prompt. No verification code. No notification he noticed.

He changed his password immediately, which was the right instinct and completely useless, because his password was never the thing that got stolen.

Here's the anatomy of the whole thing.

The Cookie
Every time you log into a website, the site hands your browser a small file and says: keep this. It's how you stay logged in. Without it you'd type your password every time you refreshed.

That file is the login. Not a record of the login — the thing itself. Present a valid cookie and Instagram doesn't ask who you are, it just opens the door and lets you in, because you're carrying the key.

The cookie is not your password. It's better than your password. It works without being typed, without being remembered, and without a single prompt you could spot.

Your logged-in state is an object sitting on your hard drive. Objects can be copied.

The Download
The installer Rahul ran was a real copy of the software with something riding along inside it.

Not a virus in the dramatic sense. An infostealer — small, quiet, single-purpose. These don't pop up windows or slow your machine down. They open your browser's cookie store, scoop out the files, and upload them somewhere.

The upload took under a second on his connection. He noticed nothing.

The files sat in a stranger's folder for weeks, matching his machine to his logins to his Instagram handle. When they finally used it, the cookie was still valid, because Instagram sessions don't expire on a timer that anyone checks. They expire when you log out — and Rahul never logged out. Nobody does.

Free software is free because someone found another way to get paid.

The Café
The Wi-Fi in that café was called Cafe_Rahul_Loves — or close enough.

There was a second network in the list too, Cafe_Free_WiFi, with a full signal and no password. He connected to that one, because it was easier and it looked official.

It was a laptop in a bag two tables over.

An evil twin is just a hotspot with a convincing name. When you join it, the attacker sits between you and the internet. Every page you load passes through their machine first, and they can serve you a copy of any page they like — including a login screen that isn't the one you think you're on.

Rahul logged into his email on that network. On the fake copy he'd been handed, the password went to the laptop in the bag.

A Wi-Fi network's name is a claim, not a fact. Anyone can make a claim.

The Extension
The unfollower extension asked for permission to "read and change all your data on all websites you visit."

Rahul clicked allow, because that's what the button says and it's the only way to get the feature. It was a real extension with a real feature. It was also, quietly, sitting inside every page he loaded — including every page with a session cookie on it.

Some extensions like this get sold to new owners after they build a user base. The new owner pushes an update, and now the same permissions belong to someone with a different agenda. Nothing looks different. The icon doesn't change.

He'd given a stranger a seat inside his browser and never thought about it again.

Extension permissions are permanent until you take them back. Most people never take them back.

The Logout That Didn't
When Rahul finally noticed the login alert from a strange city, he did the obvious thing: changed his password.

The session stayed alive.

A stolen cookie doesn't care what your password is. It's already authenticated. Changing the password does not invalidate sessions that were created before the change — not unless you go and kill them yourself.

The account kept working for the attacker for another nine hours, while Rahul sat changing his password a second time and refreshing his email.

What finally worked was one button: log out of all devices. Every session token died at once, including the stolen one. The attacker's next refresh put them on a login screen with nothing to type.

Changing your password closes the door. Logging out of all devices locks it.

What Actually Helps
This one is more about habits than settings, because there's no toggle that fixes it.

Log out of all devices monthly. Settings → Security → Where you're logged in. It's the only reliable way to kill a copied session, and it takes fifteen seconds.
Change your password after any suspicious login AND log out everywhere. One without the other does nothing.
Don't log into anything on public Wi-Fi you don't have to. If you must, use a mobile hotspot. The café can wait.
Check the network name with staff. "Is your Wi-Fi called Cafe_Free_WiFi?" One question kills the whole evil twin.
Prune your extensions every few months. If you don't use it weekly, remove it. Check permissions when you install anything.
Buy or "acquire" software from the developer's own site where you can. Cracked installers are the single most common infostealer delivery method there is.
Rahul got his account back the same day, mostly because he was awake and fast. He kept the extension — he just read the permissions properly the second time. He stopped downloading cracked software, which lasted about five months.

He also added a rule for himself: the first thing he does after any "new login" email is hit log out of all devices, before he even reads the details.

Password change is the second step now. The lockout comes first.
