He Had a System. The System Was the Problem.
Sameer changed his password every year. Every version was guessable.
Sameer has a system, and he's proud of it.

It started in college in 2016. A forum he signed up for asked for a password with a capital letter, a number and a symbol, so he made one: Sameer@2016. Then he used it everywhere, because it passed every rule any site threw at him.

Every January he bumped the number. Sameer@2017. Sameer@2018. By 2024 he was on Sameer@2024 and he genuinely believed this made him more secure than most people. He changed it annually. He never reused an old one. He followed the rules.

The rules were the problem.

Here's how that system fell apart in about forty minutes.

The Origin
His password had three things in it: his first name, the current year, and one symbol. Every version. Unchanged in structure for eight years.

That's not a password. That's a formula with one variable.

Formulas are great for remembering and terrible for security, because an attacker doesn't need to guess your password — they only need to guess your format, and formats are much shorter to guess than passwords.

Once someone has seen one of your passwords, they've effectively seen all of them. They just don't know which year you're on.

A predictable change is barely a change at all.

The Leak
In 2019, the forum he'd signed up for in college got breached.

Two hundred thousand accounts. Emails, passwords, in plain text. The dump circulated for a while, then got folded into bigger collections, then stopped being news.

sameer.k@… — Sameer@2016.

Eight years later he has no memory of that forum. He has no memory of the password either, because he changed it the following January, like he always did.

But the 2016 version was sitting in a file on the internet, labelled with his email address, free to download.

Old accounts don't expire. Neither do their leaks.

The List
The file it got folded into is called a combo list. They're enormous — billions of email-and-password pairs, stitched together from hundreds of breaches, sorted and deduplicated and traded around like trading cards.

Nobody reads these. Scripts do. A script takes your email, finds every password ever attached to it, and tries them elsewhere.

Sameer's email had exactly one entry: Sameer@2016.

Which would be useless on its own, if he didn't have a system.

Attackers don't need your current password. They need any password you've ever had, plus a guess about your habits.

The Mutation
The script tried Sameer@2016 on a few sites. It worked on one — a shop he'd bought shoes from in 2018 and never thought about again.

Now the script had a template: name, symbol, four-digit year.

It generated the rest. Sameer@2017. Sameer@2018. All the way to Sameer@2026, plus variants — Sameer@24, Sameer1994@, Sameer@2024!. About two hundred guesses total. A script does that in under a minute.

The current one, Sameer@2024, hit on his email provider on the fourth try.

You are not competing against a person guessing. You're competing against a person's guess, multiplied by a machine.

The Brute Force Part
Where the pattern didn't apply, the script fell back on brute force — just trying things, fast.

This only works where a site lets it. Instagram is fairly well defended: too many wrong attempts and it makes you wait. But plenty of sites aren't, and the consequences land anyway, because a password that works in one place tends to work in five.

What brute force punishes most is shortness. A six-character password falls in seconds. An eight-character one with a capital and a digit falls in hours. A four-word passphrase of twenty-odd characters takes longer than the sun has left.

Complexity helps a little. Length helps enormously. The rules taught everyone to make short complicated passwords, which is the worst of both.

Length is the only thing that scales in your favour.

The Quiet Part
Nobody touched his Instagram.

They went to his email, logged in with Sameer@2024, and hit "forgot password" on Instagram from there. The reset link landed in an inbox they now owned.

Instagram did everything right. It sent the code to the verified address, exactly as designed. The verified address just belonged to someone else that afternoon.

Sameer never got a suspicious login alert on Instagram, because there was nothing suspicious to alert — as far as Instagram could tell, it was him.

By the time he noticed, the recovery email had been swapped. He was locked out of an account whose password he knew perfectly well.

Your email is the master key. Every account you own is only as strong as it.

What Actually Helps
This is the easiest one to fix permanently, and the fix takes an afternoon.

Use a password manager. It generates random 20-character passwords you never memorise, so the formula problem disappears entirely. You only remember one strong master password.
Long beats clever. If you insist on memorising, use four random words — plum-tractor-canyon-lamp. Twenty-plus characters, easy to type, hard to guess.
Never reuse, not once. A single reused password is a thread that pulls the whole wardrobe down. Use the manager and stop deciding.
Check haveibeenpwned.com. Search your email. Every password that shows up in a breach gets changed today.
Secure the email first. 2FA on the inbox, unique password, before anything else. Every other account hangs off it.
Turn on passkeys where offered. No password to leak, no formula to guess.
Stop changing passwords on a schedule. Change them on a breach, not on a calendar. Annual rotation is what created the @2024 problem in the first place.
Sameer's password manager now holds forty-one entries, all random, none of which he could recite. He knows one password and it's a passphrase of five words that he types badly every time.

He says the worst part wasn't losing the account for two days. It's that he thought he was the careful one.

He was following every rule he'd ever been given. The rules were written for a world where attackers had to remember things too.
