Your Password Is Not the Lock. Your Inbox Is.
A story about the one account nobody bothers to protect
Nadia runs a vintage clothing shop out of a storage unit in Leeds and posts the stock to Instagram. It's a good page — 5,800 followers, decent engagement, a lot of repeat customers who message her directly.

Her email is nadia_vintage@yahoo.com. She made it in 2006. She uses it for the shop, the supplier invoices, the domain, the lot.

She is careful with her Instagram password. She changes it whenever the app nags her. It's a proper one — fourteen characters, no name in it, nothing to do with the shop.

Her Yahoo password is vintage2006.

That's the whole story, really. But the route from vintage2006 to a hijacked shop account is worth walking through, because nobody expects it to be this short.

The Front Door Nobody Locks
Nadia thinks of Instagram as the thing worth protecting. It's the shop, the audience, the income.

She thinks of the Yahoo account as plumbing. Something things pass through. She checks it twice a day for orders and never once considered it a security surface.

But when she taps "forgot password" on Instagram, the reset code doesn't go to her phone. It goes to that inbox. Instagram isn't protecting the account from the password side — it's protecting it from the email side, and it assumes the email side is already solid.

It wasn't. It had a password a stranger could guess in an afternoon.

The reset button doesn't ask how strong your Instagram password is. It asks who controls your inbox.

The Email in the Bio
The attacker found her email the way a customer would.

It's in her bio, in the "for bookings" line, and on the contact page of her shop link. She'd put it there for wholesale enquiries, because that's how wholesale enquiries work.

nadia_vintage@yahoo.com.

And her handle is nadia_vintage.

Same string again. The address and the account, sitting next to each other, both public, both hers, one of them weak.

The attacker didn't need to research her. She'd drawn a line from the account to the door and labelled both.

A public contact address is a public attack surface. That's the trade you make for being reachable.

The Guess
Yahoo has been breached so many times that Nadia's address was almost certainly in a dump already. Her password may well have been too.

The attacker tried the two or three leaked strings attached to that address. None worked — she'd changed it after a warning email in 2019.

Then they tried the obvious. Nadia2006. nadia_vintage1. Vintage2006!. vintage2006.

The last one worked. It had worked for nineteen years.

No brute force needed, no phishing page, no malware. An afternoon of guessing against an account that had never had a second factor, protecting a password that was the shop's name and the year she started it.

Old inboxes collect weak passwords the way drawers collect cables. Nobody upgrades them because nothing has broken yet.

The Reset
She was at a supplier's warehouse, phone in a coat pocket, when it happened. 2pm on a Wednesday.

The attacker opened Instagram, typed her address, tapped forgot password. The code arrived in her Yahoo inbox. They read it there.

Ninety seconds, start to finish. Instagram sent her a "new login" notification, which she saw later and assumed was her own doing.

Then they changed her recovery email. Then they turned on 2FA against their own number. Then they logged every session out — hers included — and she was on the wrong side of the door with no key.

A recovery flow is not a fallback. It's the primary route, and it runs through your email.

The Mining
While they were in there, they read.

Her birthday, in the caption of a post about a 1970s dress. Her hometown, in a tagged location. Her old school, mentioned in a Story she'd saved to a highlight. Her dog, in forty-one photos — the dog's name was also the answer to her Yahoo security question.

Most of that was for the recovery questions on the mail provider, which she'd set up in 2006 and never reviewed. Her mother's maiden name was the answer to another, and it was in a comment on her sister's anniversary post from 2021.

None of it was oversharing. It was just her life, on a public page, for nineteen years.

You aren't publishing data. You're publishing the answers to the questions that prove you're you.

The Facebook
There was a second way in, and it was older than the shop.

Her Facebook was linked to the Instagram. Made in 2008, same password family, last posted in 2022. When the attacker changed the recovery email, they unlinked it on the way through, so the obvious fallback disappeared behind them.

They didn't need it. But it was there, and it was open, and it had the same lock on it as the Yahoo account.

Nadia had not looked at that Facebook in two years. She'd have told you it was dead.

Every account you've walked away from is still standing there with the password you left on it.

Getting It Back
She noticed at 6pm, when she tried to post and got a login screen.

The reset link went to an address she didn't recognise. The Facebook route was gone. She filed a support ticket and got an automated reply.

What got her back was a video selfie and a screenshot of a 2006 Yahoo confirmation email she'd never deleted — the "welcome to Instagram" one, still sitting in an inbox folder called shop stuff.

Three days. She got it on Saturday. By then the account had posted four "clearance" stories with a payment link, and eleven customers had paid a total of £340 into an account that wasn't hers.

She refunded all of them.

The receipt from the day you signed up is the most useful thing in your inbox. Almost nobody keeps it.

What Actually Helps
The fix is entirely upstream. Do these in this order.

Put 2FA on the email before anything else. Authenticator app. Everything you own hangs off that inbox.
Change the old email password to something long and random. Not the shop name. Not the year. A password manager generates it and you never type it.
Use a separate address for the account. One that isn't in your bio, isn't on your link page, and doesn't match your handle.
Review your recovery questions and lie if you want to. Mother's maiden name is public. So is your school. A nonsense answer written down beats an honest one you've posted about.
Unlink the dead Facebook, or lock it down. A dormant profile is a second door with a 2014 lock.
Never delete the welcome email. Star it. It's proof of ownership that no attacker can fake.
Add recovery contacts and passkeys. Both route around the inbox completely.
Nadia changed the Yahoo password to twenty-three random characters and put an authenticator app on it. She also set up a separate address for the shop that appears nowhere on the page.

She kept the public contact email, because a shop needs one. It's just no longer the same one the account recovers through.

She says the bit that stings is that she thought she'd been careful. She had a good Instagram password. She'd never clicked anything dodgy.

She'd just never once thought of her email as part of her Instagram. Nobody does. That's why it keeps working.
